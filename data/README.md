# Synthetic catalog and relevance labels

`catalog.json` contains 54 invented products across six categories (9 each): electronics, home, outdoors, office, fitness, and pets. Names, descriptions, attributes, and prices were written for this demonstration with Codex assistance on 2026-10-26. No store was scraped, no public retail dataset was copied, and no customer, sales, behavioral, or private data is included. Prices use integer cents and fictional USD amounts. Products, specifications, and prices are not real offers or independently verified product claims.

`queries.json` contains 30 author-created search intents: 10 `dev` queries and 20 `heldout` queries. The held-out set was written and fixed before running either retrieval method's evaluation. It is a small internal check, not an independent benchmark. Query writers and implementation share a project context, so judgment bias and catalog familiarity are real limitations. No click logs or human user study support these labels.

Each query has a stable ID, `query`, `relevant_ids`, `split`, and a short `rationale`. Optional `filters` use a category slug and inclusive `min_price` / `max_price` values in decimal USD. Relevance is binary: a product either meets the intent or does not. Labels describe useful answers after applying the explicit filters. Related accessories are excluded when they do not satisfy the requested product or purpose. Broad intents can admit multiple relevant products (for example, D07 accepts both drink bottles under 25 USD). The set includes natural-language needs, a broad ambiguous intent, misspellings, category/budget constraints, and one no-answer case per split.

No-answer queries intentionally have empty `relevant_ids`. MRR and recall require separate handling for those queries; they should not be assigned perfect recall or blended into answerable-query metrics. Report their false-positive behavior separately. Judgments are approximate and incomplete outside these 30 queries. They should be reviewed and expanded before using this app to make claims about retrieval quality on a real catalog.
